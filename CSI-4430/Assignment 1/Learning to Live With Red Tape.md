# It can tie up projects unduly, yet the organization man (or woman) can use it wisely 

Do you hate red tape? Do you consider it a point of honor to frustrate it, bypass it or simply ignore it? If you do, you have lots of company -- and most of you are probably heading for a great fall. If you think, "I could run this organization better and without the red tape," read on. 

Having coped with company rules, procedures, and policies -- so-called red tape -- from both engineering and managerial positions, I am well aware of the drawbacks -- and benefits -- of red tape. True, it does tend to grow in layers. It is misused by first line supervisors sometimes. It may stifle creative initiative on occasion. But if you step back and take an impartial view, that red tape is not without redeeming features. 

In most cases company rules protect you and the company. Learn to work with the red tape instead of fighting it continually, and you will often get results as quickly as you could working around it -- and without unexpected risk to you and the company. 

Way back when certain Zener diodes were still the newest gadgets on the market, I was hit with a problem: to eliminate transients that were causing random transistor failures on aircraft fuel-level indicators. I devised a circuit using the diodes and sought out a test aircraft for my breadboards. Normally a chain of company approvals provided protection against hasty design, but I was so enamored of the test circuit and the immediate availability of the test aircraft, a crew training plane, that I bypassed protective procedures and sold my idea to the top man in the approval chain. This was possible because he was too high up to do more than accept my technical judgment. 

During the next training flight the overtaxed Zeners blew, and what would have been a random failure of instruments became instead a short to ground of half the fuel-level indicators. I had forgotten to protect against failure of the protectors. I learned the value of those company approvals the hard way. 

Critics don't limit their denunciations of red tape to the need for approvals. They denounce any proceduralism that delays and frustrates them. In particular, engineers become terribly upset when anything diverts them from their goal of "results." Everything from time records to the supervisory hierarchy is condemned as red tape. 

But take care before you denounce the system; it has been built up gradually, and neither apathy nor ignorance keeps it in place. Procedures can serve you if you let them, and familiarity in working with them can yield effective results. 

Let's look first at how "rules and regulations" -- the grist of red tape -- fit into an organization's modus operandi. 
## Rules can be useful
In a modern organization formal authority is linked to a position, but informal power attaches to a person. Most organizations are staffed by people who cannot, or will not, combine the two efficiently enough to assure a steady flow of work and protect the organization's long-term interests. Hence the organization must create rules to regulate work flow and to discipline those who deviate from the norm. The organization must then determine whether these controls are working, and it does so by creating additional checkpoints and reporting procedures. 

Consumers, clients, competitors -- the whole world -- generate a hierarchy of politics and rules. The organization must protect its interests from these external forces, and the very least it will do is to create procedures for response to queries. For example, an "innocent" inquiry to the company engineering department may mask an attorney's probing for weaknesses in a product liability suit. Policies and procedures forcing a central legal review not only provide protection but also prevent an outsider from probing. 

Rules perform other functions. They are the equivalent of direct, personal orders and thus relieve the manager of the burden of specifying over and over again what is expected of employees. Rules perform a screening function, limiting the supervisor's contact with subordinates. They limit the employee's scope for action without constant referral to the boss. They permit the employee to submit to the organization when personalities might otherwise render that awkward. 

Rules facilitate communication -- people who deviate from them cannot claim ignorance of their obligations; they generally conform without need for close supervision. Rules legitimize punishment, but they do so with built-in "leeway." The engineer who is criticized for failing to conform to written company rules may feel far less the victim of unjust harassment than one who operates in an environment of ad hoc rule-making. 

Rules, then, can depersonalize management's interactions with employees in "sticky" or abrasive areas. They offer the employee an impersonal target that is "bigger than all of us." In many cases they promote better relations between supervisor and subordinate than is found in organizations where management is not bound by company rules. 

But professional people intent on getting a job done are usually highly vocal critics of red tape. To them, it is a pathology -- a deviation from what they perceive as the proper way to do things. If you are in this category, be cautious before you mount your attack. Be aware that in your discovery of red-tape pathology in your organization, you are not original. There are researchers of organizational operations who have even minutely classified such pathologies.

The most frequently noted criticism of organizational red tape is that it creates resistance to change and consequently stifles innovation. This judgment may not be entirely fair. Controls preventing quick decisions or sudden changes may be labeled red tape by disappointed proponents of the changes, but the hierarchy may view the controls as protection against foolishness. The ideal organization is a critically damped system in which ill-considered or unimportant transients are damped out, while other signals cause the appropriate response. At an early stage in the life of an organization, an engineer can walk into the vice president's office, sketch an idea on a pad, and walk out with a decision. A mature organization, on the other hand, requires project forms, profit analyses, and capital funding projections. 

The difference is not a result of hardening of the corporate arteries or intent. At a corporation's maturity, its owners, investors, and management are past the do-or-die, win-or-lose acceptance stage; the stakes are now higher. The result is that the mature company encourages product improvements whose financial payoff can be reasonably forecast, as opposed to innovations whose cost and profitability are unclear. 

A second organizational pathology involves means-ends reversal and confusion between needs and wants. For instance, the paper statements of goals are not ends; they are the means by which efforts are appropriately focused. The ends are the products moving out the door, the dividends flowing to stockholders, and the increase in stockholders' equity. Means will be mistaken for ends if adherence to procedure becomes primary. A good manager maintains a proper relationship between organizational goals and the product coming off the assembly line. 

The emotional cost of pathological red tape can be high in both time and effort. In one typical case, a federal agency forced an employee to take early retirement by requiring him to "go by the book," even though his performance was otherwise satisfactory. He was directed to fill out forms and follow every step in a "management by objectives" manual, which was poorly written, complex, and frequently ignored by other employees. After a while he threw in the towel and retired, as his superiors apparently hoped he'd do. He had no other choice, except to risk a nervous breakdown. 

The shifting downward of emotional costs by those with authority is a third organizational pathology. The shift may be accompanied by centralization of all decision -- making-everything from dollar spending to how many pads an engineer may draw from supply must be cleared "through the boss." The engineer at the bottom cannot shift the emotional costs further down, and valuing his job too much to try to reverse them upward, he may discharge his feelings on spouse and children or beat up the family dog. This phenomenon has spawned an enormous interest in stress-management courses and self-help books. 

The personal pathologies of red tape strike hardest probably at the first line of supervision. The first-line manager, who will be called on the carpet in the event of failure, has the closest responsibility for the output of the staff. To protect himself, this manager may make subordinates comply with all rules and even redefine the organization's goals so that his staff avoids failure. The first-line manager tries hard to present a positive image to the hierarchy. Subordinates, in turn, may view the "game" as avoiding black marks; decisions by precedence are deemed safer than individual initiative, and sitting tight is preferred to rocking the boat. A manager seeking to motivate such an organization may make the mistake of assuming that there are not enough procedures to get people moving. The result may be even more red tape and a further crushing of initiative.

In the extreme, rituals of red tape can paralyze an organization, and I've seen this happen in a university. Two researchers I know were so frustrated by the university paperwork and approvals required for a grant that they took their talents elsewhere. 

Red-tape pathology may lead to unreasoning rebellion by employees who are frustrated, and this may damage a person's career more than the pathology itself justifies. In the first place, the person who proclaims that red tape is unnecessary may have only a limited view of what is essential to the long-term existence of the organization. Second, those who denounce red tape most vehemently often are personally insecure and tense; they dislike waiting for results and often have an immature outlook when it comes to investing their energies in projects -- they want immediate payoffs. They complain that "the organization wants conformers" and that "only pull will get you anywhere." All too often they fail to learn why red tape exists. Consequently they violate all the rules (not only the unnecessary or excessive constraints), refuse to keep required records, and increase the workload of others. 

These complainers express a desire for a simpler, less restricted relationship with the hierarchy and a return to the personal touch of the "good old days," when the organization accepted things "on faith" and allowed people to "push them through" on the chance that the results would be worth the risk. Management tends to view such feelings as rationalizations based on lack of personal success and may view the critic as alienated and immature, unable to wait as the large, complex organization moves with all deliberate speed on a project or a change. In short, the critic of red tape becomes just as much the object of investigation as the situation that is allegedly being sabotaged by the red tape. 
## Living with red tape 
So how does one live with red tape? Learn where, how, and why that red tape was created. Study and chart communications channels, work-flow channels, and the red tape that governs them. Establish, in effect, a personal operating manual dealing with both the formal and informal organization within your company or department. 

Analyze key positions that influence your own advancement in the organization. Analyze the occupants of those positions. Then become a key component of their informal networks by:
- Protecting them from their own and others' mistakes. Learn their problems and goals and do what you can to provide remedies as needed and without being asked. 
- Providing assistance where it can be volunteered. Find out who is on the corporate committee, who writes the committee reports, and offer support to those who could be of use to you in the future. 
- Providing expertise and projecting an image of cooperation. 
- Taking care of as many procedures as possible without being told. I teach at a university and my students provide me with a stamped, self-addressed envelope at the end of each term so they can obtain confirmation that they have completed the course and so receive tuition refunds from their employers. Why don't they also provide me with an insert showing their names, Social Security numbers, and the course title and number, so that all I have to do is fill in the grades and sign, instead of reading the envelope and searching out the appropriate records? 

Next establish checklists for maintaining contacts with those whose opinions should be solicited, those whose clearance approvals should be sought, and those who should be kept informed-in all three cases, if only as a courtesy. The smart organization person works his or her way through red tape by contacting those whose approval is not needed but who want to keep in touch with what is going on. The result? An informal network of contacts that is lubricated by frequent "oiling."

Proceeding without authorization to get the job done is highly touted in novels and in movies, The hero succeeds, and the company climbs to new heights of sales and prestige. We never hear of the failures by the wayside. 

But there may come a time when red tape is better cut than followed. What, you may ask, is one to do when the red tape process does interfere with an important and time-constrained assignment? The problem is learning to distinguish between apparent "emergencies" and real ones. When red tape is cut, the risks are generally enormous. As one who was burned, may I offer some suggestions? If you choose to cut through red tape: 
- Maintain the attitude publicly that you are doing it for the job only -- because of time, importance, and the like. Don't let yourself be branded as a rebel against the system.
- Work through your supervisor and get tacit approval for what you are doing.
- Make absolutely sure that you have all the facts. Is the item really that important for you to put yourself in jeopardy and your colleagues through the strains of this violation of routine? Or is it really only your interpretation, your personal scale of values, that makes this matter nonroutine? I should have asked these questions when I cut the red-tape approvals for my Zener diode circuits. Had I been less headstrong, had I searched out all the facts, I could have avoided trouble.
- Develop a standing plan for yourself that prescribes how you will cut red tape should the occasion arise. This will pre- vent you from "shooting from the hip."
- Evaluate carefully the upside rewards and the downside costs in dollars, in time, in terms of your reputation, and in terms of what you will really accomplish by circumventing the red tape. Don't win the battle and lose the war, thereby undermining your career promotional opportunities. 

Why the overall tone of approval for red tape? I have had experience on both sides of the hierarchy. From the engineering side, I have stormed against red tape and have cut it to accomplish what I felt was a greater good for the organization. But then I had to stand the heat when the greater good had broader negative ramifications and my boss was roasted for allowing me to cut the red tape. Now I am sitting on the managerial side in my own company. I, too, am manufacturing red tape. Why? 

Simply put, not everyone who works for a company has his or her heart, mind or soul on the work. Even the best employees go to work at times with family problems and a sleepless night behind them. Without checklists and procedures, mistakes happen. A steam boiler may be wired so its electrical pressure controls are inadvertently bypassed, checks are not performed, and serious problems arise. 

Red tape, in short, can help to ensure that what comes out of an organization is correct, can withstand all the legal ramifications, and meets the needs of the organization over the long term. It assures that a simple response to a customer does not become the basis for a liability suit or that a simple meeting with another company's representative does not lead to a collusion-in-pricing lawsuit. 

Corporate reform from the bottom is rare; it must come from the top. But I'm willing to bet that when you reach the top, you will undo very few of the red-tape controls you attacked from the bottom. They really do serve a function in most cases. And if they don't, get the brass to reform them.
